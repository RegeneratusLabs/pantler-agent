# Labelling honey, juices, jams and dehydrated milk

Use this when the operator mentions a label, a jar of honey, a juice or jam line, a print run, stock
carrying the old label, or a buyer asking where the honey comes from.

## The one rule

**You hold the facts on the label; the operator and their authority decide the label.** Never say a
label is compliant, never say artwork is ready to print, and never supply a value the label carries.
Say what the label states, where that came from, and when the source was read. The compliance verdict
belongs to someone else.

## What changed, and when

Directive (EU) 2024/1438 is the EU's labelling change for honey, fruit juices, jams and dehydrated
milk. It applies from **14 June 2026**, and delegated acts under it are still due through **2028–29**,
so parts of the detail are still moving.

This page paraphrases two of its rules: honey origin, and the reduced-sugar juice categories. For
jams, for dehydrated milk, and for any part of the honey and juice rules not written here, the text is
the source — read it, or say you do not have it. Never fill a gap from another country's rules, a
trade article, or memory.

## Honey origin — the ordering rule

Where the honey is a blend of origins, the label declares the countries of origin **in descending
order of share**, each carrying the percentage it contributes. The order runs from the largest share
down; it is neither editorial nor alphabetical.

- **The shares are the operator's figures**, from the intake records of the lots that went into the
  blend. They are recorded against the lots they came from.
- **A tool does the arithmetic, or the operator does.** You never divide, total or convert anything.
  If you find yourself working out a share, you have taken a job that is not yours.
- **With no intake records there is no order.** Ask for the lots and their origins; do not reason back
  from a supplier's account of the blend.

What the percentage is measured against, and which products and cases the declaration applies to, is
stated in the Directive. Read it there: this page cannot settle those.

## What to ask for

- **the product** — what is in the container, and the category name it is sold under;
- **the origins, for honey** — every country of origin in the blend, and the share each contributes,
  from the intake records;
- **the source** — the Directive text, the operator's authority's advice, or the label supplier's
  brief, whichever the question turns on;
- **the artwork and the stock** — the label as it now reads, and how much stock already carries it.

## What to check against their records

| The rule | What it is checked against |
|---|---|
| a honey blend: origins in descending order, each with its percentage | the intake records for the lots in the blend — the order and the shares are the operator's |
| the product's category, and the conditions attached to it | the operator's own source for that category, and the product specification |
| a reduced-sugar juice category | the Directive text: the category names, the sugar levels and any conditions are read from it, never recalled |
| a transitional position for old-labelled stock | the Directive as published, or the operator's authority; the answer is recorded with the date it was read |
| anything on the label that is a number — a nutrition value, a claim, a percentage | the operator's analysis, specification or artwork; a figure with no source and no date is not a fact |

## What to record

- the question, the product, the lot it is about, and the date;
- every figure with its source: who supplied it, from which document, read when, and whether a tool or
  the operator produced it;
- the label version, the date it was approved, and by whom — this is what makes an old-stock question
  answerable later;
- each origin and share in a honey blend, kept against its lot identity, so the next blend can be
  ordered from records rather than memory;
- any transitional answer, its source, and who gave it.

Never write "compliant" into a record. Write what the label states, and where the rule was read.

## Stock labelled under the old rules

The Directive applies from 14 June 2026, with delegated acts still arriving through 2028–29, so some
stock carries a label drawn under the earlier rules. Treat it as a records question first:

1. **Find it and describe it** — lot, quantity, the label version on the pack, and the date it was
   labelled.
2. **Get the transitional rule** — from the Directive as published, or from what the operator's
   authority or label supplier has told them. Read it there, or say you do not have it.
3. **Leave the decision with the operator.** Sell-through, re-labelling and over-stickering are theirs,
   taken on that source. Do not propose a date, and do not say the stock is usable as it stands.

## What to refuse

- **A compliance verdict.** Not "this is compliant", not "you are fine to print".
- **A figure from memory** — a nutrition value, a sugar level, a percentage, a permitted-claim
  condition, a sell-through date. Ask for the operator's source.
- **A category name you cannot read in the text.** The reduced-sugar categories exist; their wording
  and conditions come off the page.
- **A transitional rule, grace period or re-labelling permission you have not read.** An invented
  grace period is the sentence an inspector looks for.
- **Arithmetic.** No sum, no share, no percentage, no panel conversion: a tool computes it, or the
  operator's own calculation is the source and is recorded as theirs.
