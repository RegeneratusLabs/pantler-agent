# Organic certification records

Use this when the operator mentions certification, an inspection, a renewal, a certificate, an input
approval, a conversion period, a buffer zone, or a supplier's organic status.

Two jobs, and they are different: **your records** — what happened on this farm, which you can answer
from what the operator has told you — and **the standard** — what the rules say, which you may only
answer from a document you have in front of you. Sort every question into one of the two before
answering, and say which one you are answering.

## The one rule

**You hold the records; the certifier certifies.** Never state, imply or repeat that the business, a
product, a block or a supplier *is* certified. Say what the records show and when they were made. If
the operator asks whether they are compliant, the answer is a record, not an assurance.

## Records are made when the work happens

A record reconstructed the week before an audit is worth less than one made on the day, and an
inspector can tell the difference. When the operator describes work, offer to write it down now.

| The duty | The record it leaves |
|---|---|
| Anything applied to land or crop — sprays, amendments, fertigation | product, rate, area or block, date, operator, and the withholding or re-entry period |
| Anything bought in — seed, seedlings, stock, inputs, feed | supplier, product, quantity, date, and the supplier's certificate where the input is a certified one |
| Anything harvested | block or lot, date, quantity, and the lot identity every later record hangs from |
| Anything sold or dispatched | customer, lot identity, quantity, date, and where it went |
| Cleaning, sanitation and pest control | what, where, when, and what it was |
| Complaints, returns and anything recalled | what happened, what was done, and when |
| Workers and contractors | who worked, when, and any induction or training the scheme requires |
| The certificate itself | the certifier, the scope, the certificate number, and the expiry date |

The **audit trail** is the chain these make together: purchase → storage → application → harvest →
sale, per block, each step dated and pointing at the one before it. When the operator asks what an
inspector will want, the honest answer is usually "the chain, complete, and made at the time".

## Input approvals — a three-way answer, never a yes

An input is allowed because the operator's own standard says so — not because it seems organic, and
not because another country allows it. Answer in exactly one of three ways:

- **Allowed** — and you name the clause you read it in.
- **Not allowed** — and you name the clause.
- **Uncertain — this is one for your certifier** — when the product is not in the standard you have,
  when the standard's wording is conditional, or when you only have a summary of it.

That third answer is a real answer and the correct one more often than it feels. **Never approve an
input on your own judgement.** "It's probably fine" is the sentence that loses a certification.

Two traps that look like help and are not: a product permitted in another jurisdiction is not
permitted here, and an input the operator has used for years is not thereby allowed.

## Conversion and in-conversion produce

Conversion runs from the last prohibited input or practice on that parcel — so the fact to capture is
**that date, per block**, and then elapsed time against the period the operator's standard states for
their case. Periods differ by land use, by crop and by livestock, and they are scheme-specific: read
the period from the source, and never generalise one you have seen for another situation.

Keep the produce separate and labelled while it is in conversion. Selling in-conversion produce as
organic is the mistake the whole record set exists to prevent.

## Buffer zones

A buffer is a separation or management zone protecting the certified area from contamination from
adjacent land. Two things follow, and only one of them is a number:

1. **The reasoning is yours to do with the operator**: what is next to the block, which way the water
   and the wind go, what the neighbour sprays, how wide the physical separation actually is. Record
   what is there, on a date.
2. **The minimum width comes from the standard.** If you have not read it, you do not know it.

**There is no small-farm waiver unless the operator's own standard grants one.** "You may waive this
on a small holding" is a fabricated exemption, and it is the exact sentence that motivated
[`cite-or-refuse`](../cite-or-refuse/SKILL.md).

## The renewal calendar

Certification runs on a cycle: the certificate expiry, the inspection, any interim report, and the
fee. When the operator gives you a date, record it and set the reminder. When they have not, ask for
the certificate rather than guessing — an invented deadline is the same failure as an invented rule.

How often inspections fall due is scheme-dependent. Do not state a frequency from memory.

## Supplier certificates

Hold the certificate as a record, note its scope and its expiry, and check that the scope covers what
is actually being bought. An expired certificate is worth saying out loud, early.

Most schemes keep no open register — the registries are closed or awkward — so supplier verification
is an **operator attestation with a link**, not a lookup. Record the evidence the operator has, and
label it as theirs.

## Before an inspection

Walk the chain, not the calendar: for each block, does purchase lead to storage to application to
harvest to sale without a missing link; does every applied input trace to a label or an approval; are
the certificates current; are the in-conversion parcels marked. Produce the list of what is missing —
that is more useful than reassurance, and it is the only kind of answer this skill gives.

## When you do not know

Say so, and say what would settle it — which document, which section, and whether the operator can
hand you the file. The standards map, and which documents may be quoted at all, is in
[`references/standards-map.md`](references/standards-map.md).
