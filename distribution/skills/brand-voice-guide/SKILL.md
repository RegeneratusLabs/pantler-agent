# Brand voice guide

Use this when the operator wants everything they send out to sound like them — the shop page, the market
sign, the newsletter, the posts — and wants it written down so a later draft can be held to it. They will
ask for their "brand voice". What they need is one page of their own words with the claims sorted out.

## The one rule

**Every line in the guide comes from a sentence the operator wrote or said.** You arrange their words; you
do not supply a personality. A word they would not use at the stall does not go into their mouth — it goes
in the list of words that are not theirs.

A guide written without their sentences is a house style. It reads fine, it is not theirs, and every draft
held to it sounds like a stranger.

Nothing here goes anywhere by itself: the guide is a file the operator keeps, and every draft held to it is
a draft they put out themselves.

## Ask for sentences, not adjectives

Do not open with "describe your brand in three words". That question produces exactly the vocabulary this
skill exists to keep out. Ask instead for things they have already written:

- the last two or three messages they sent a customer;
- the stall sign, the chalkboard, the label draft, the note in the box;
- a note they dictated about this week's orders or the field walk;
- anything they wrote by hand and were pleased with.

Eight to ten real sentences is enough, and at least one of them written in a hurry — the hurried ones are
where the voice is. If they would rather talk than type, let them talk for a minute, write down what they
said, and read it back for corrections before a line of it goes in the guide.

## Read back what is actually there

Name what the sentences show, not what the operator says about themselves:

- **the words they use for things** — their names for the produce, the boxes, the blocks, the customers.
  Those are the names their records use too, and a draft that renames them sounds like somebody else;
- **I or we**, and whether it changes when they are pleased or annoyed;
- **how long the sentences run**, and how they open and close;
- **what is missing** — the words the sentences never use. The absences are as useful as the presences.

## Three tiers of vocabulary

This is the part that makes the guide worth keeping. Every word it lists sits in one tier, and a word only
moves up when a record exists.

| Tier | What sits in it | What it means for a draft |
|---|---|---|
| **allowed** | their own work, named plainly — the produce, the place, the method as it was actually done, the day it was picked | write it, in their words |
| **needs evidence** | organic, spray-free, regenerative, local, native, pasture-raised, chemical-free, and any origin or environmental word | not until a record is named and read — the certificate record with its number and expiry, the input and application records behind a spray claim, the supplier's own document |
| **banned** | health claims of any kind; superlatives and promises nobody can evidence; another business's mark or scheme badge | never, whatever the operator says about it |

Two things to say out loud when you build the table. The **banned** tier holds health claims because
nothing here holds the evidence one would need, so this skill emits none. And a vague green word is not the
safe fallback: EmpCo, Directive (EU) 2024/825, names generic environmental claims as an unfair commercial
practice from 27 September 2026 — the instrument to cite is that one, not the Green Claims Directive, which
is blocked.

The claim lint is a tool now: call `claim_check` on a draft before you offer it. It refuses a
health claim outright and names the record that would have to stand behind a certification or
environmental one. This table is what you write *with*; the lint is what you check against.

## Write the guide

One page, and nothing in it that the sentences do not support. A shape, not a template:

1. who is talking — the business name as the operator writes it, and I or we;
2. six to ten sentences of theirs, quoted exactly, each with where it came from;
3. the words that are theirs, with the sense they use them in;
4. the words that are not — and where each one came from: a supplier's leaflet, another farm's sign, a word
   they say they cannot stand;
5. the three tiers, with the record each **needs evidence** word would need;
6. one line for when there is no word for something: ask, do not fill.

Mark it `draft`, date it, and name the sentences it was built from. Tell the operator it becomes theirs when
they say so, and write that date down too — a guide nobody has read is not a guide a later draft can be held
to. Keep it under a page: a guide that runs long is a guide nobody holds a draft to, which defeats the job.

## When a later draft is held to it

Quote the guide's own line back and name it: "your guide says no exclamation marks; the draft has three."
Then offer the fix in their words, not yours.

If the operator wants a new draft to break their own guide, the guide changes first — one line, dated, with
the sentences behind it — and then the draft follows. Do not let a draft set a new precedent quietly, and do
not rewrite the guide to match whatever was written last.

## What to refuse, and what to say

**No sentences of theirs yet.**

> "I can write this, but not from nothing. Send me two messages you sent a customer this week and a photo of
> the stall sign, and the guide will be yours. Without them I'd be inventing a tone, and you'd end up
> rewriting everything that came out of it."

**A claim they want listed as allowed.**

> "'Spray-free' can go in the allowed tier the moment there's an input or spray record that shows it. Until
> then it sits in needs evidence, and I won't write it into the guide as settled."

**A health claim.**

> "I don't write health claims. There's no evidence file to check one against, so they stay banned and drafts
> made from this guide won't carry them."

**A brand story with facts you do not hold** — a founding year, a grandfather's orchard, the first market
stall.

> "That's a good story and I don't have any of it. Tell me the year and who was there and I'll write it down
> as your words, sourced to you. What I won't do is invent the details around it."

**A voice for somebody else's business**, or for something that is not a business at all.

> "That's not your business's voice, so it isn't this job. Point me at the sentences and I'll do theirs."

## Before you hand it over

- the guide, as a block they can copy and keep;
- each quoted sentence with where it came from;
- every **needs evidence** word with the record it would need in order to move up;
- every word you refused, and why;
- where the guide is kept — nothing in the installed packs holds one, so it is a file in the operator's own
  keeping, and the handover names it;
- their part: read it, change anything that does not sound like them, and say when it is theirs.
