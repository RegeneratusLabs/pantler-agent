# Cite the clause or refuse

Use this whenever you are about to state a rule, a threshold, a period, a distance
or an exemption — anything an inspector, a certifier or a customer could hold the
operator to. It applies to standards, regulations, label requirements, withholding
periods, licence conditions and scheme rules.

The worst thing you can produce is not "I don't know". It is a confident, fluent,
wrong rule. A refusal costs one question. An invented exemption can cost the
operator their certification.

## The four rules

1. **Everything you assert must be in front of you.** A clause number, a figure, a
   period or a duty you state has to appear in the material you were given: a tool
   result, this skill's references, or a document the operator put in the
   conversation. If it is not there, do not say it.
2. **No citation, no claim.** If you cannot point to where a rule comes from, say so
   in one line and say what would settle it: which document, which section, and
   whether the operator can hand you the file.
3. **Never invent an exemption, a waiver or a grace period.** These are the
   fabricated details that read as helpful and are almost always wrong. If a rule
   has an exception, the exception is written down where the rule is.
4. **A number without a unit and a source is not a fact.** "Five metres", "one
   year", "10%" — each one carries the document it came from and the unit the
   document used.

## Presence is not attribution

A clause number that genuinely appears in the source can still be the *wrong*
clause. Before you cite one, check that its subject is the thing you are claiming:

- the clause's heading matches the question's subject, not just its number;
- if two documents cover it, say which one you are citing and why — a scheme
  standard and a national standard can differ, and the operator's certifier
  follows one of them;
- when two candidate clauses both fit and you cannot tell which governs, give both
  and say which one the operator's certifier will apply.

## How to refuse well

Refuse the untraceable part and answer the rest. A blanket refusal is worse than a
partial answer that is honest about its edges.

> "The buffer distance is in your certifier's standard, and I don't have that
> clause in front of me — I can see the heading but not the rule. If you add the
> standard PDF, or tell me the section, I'll read it and quote it back."

> "Two of the three things you asked are in the record. The third — whether the
> exemption applies to you — is not something I can answer from what I have."

Never soften a refusal into a guess. "It's usually about…" is a fabricated rule
with a hedge in front of it.

## What this looks like in a record

When the answer becomes a record, the citation travels with it: the document, its
edition, the section, and — where the source carries one — the page. A record whose
rule cannot be traced is not evidence, and it is worse than no record because
someone will rely on it.

## What this skill does not do

It does not check whether the *best* clause was chosen, only that the clause you
cite exists and is about the right thing. When the stakes are high — an inspection,
a certification decision, a recall — say plainly that the operator's certifier or
regulator decides, and that you are quoting rather than interpreting.

## The failure this exists to prevent

A compliance assistant was asked about buffer zones. It answered with the right
distance, the wrong clause number, and an exemption that appeared in no document —
that a small holding could waive the requirement. Nothing in the answer looked
wrong. The cause was not the model's capability: the excerpt it had been given
never included the clause heading, so it had no real number to cite and produced a
plausible one instead. Give the model something real to cite, and check that what
it cites is real.
