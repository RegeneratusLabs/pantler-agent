# Records retention

Use this when the operator asks how long to keep something, whether an old box of dockets can go out,
what an inspector or a customer could ask to see, or where a record should live.

Three kinds of record sit in the one place: **organic certification records, food-safety records and
customer orders.** They do not have the same life, and only one of the three has a period this
repository carries.

## What to ask for

- which record, and which group it belongs to — organic, food safety, or an order;
- whether the operator's own regime, certifier, scheme or accountant has stated a period for it, and
  what that period is;
- what the record is made of — the entry in the pack, a paper original, or both, and where the original
  is kept;
- whether anything live points at it: an audit, a complaint, a recall, a claim, an investigation;
- who decided it can go, if anyone has.

## What to check

- **Which duty applies.** Organic records, food-safety records and orders are three different sets of
  rules, and a period for one is not a period for another.
- **Whether a period is actually on file.** A period the operator half-remembers is not one; ask them
  to read it from their own regime, and record who said it and when.
- **Whether the record is the original or a copy.** A scan of a certificate is not the certificate, and
  the machine's `notes` saying where the paper is is not the paper.
- **Whether something live still points at it.** A lot record quoted by an open complaint is not a
  disposal candidate, whatever its date.
- **Whether a backup is being counted as a record.** A backup protects against loss; it is not a filing
  system, and a backup nobody can read is not a record.

## The one period this skill may state

**For organic records, where the operator is certified under the US National Organic Program: five
years** — and the duty is not only duration but reach, because those records must trace back to the
last certified operation (7 CFR 205.103, carried in
`skills-and-plugins.md` §4.6).

That is the only period in this repository, and it is for those records. **Every other period is
jurisdiction-specific and scheme-specific, and it comes from the operator's own regime.** Food-safety
records, customer orders and customer data, tax and employment records all differ by where the business
is and what it has signed up to, and this repository carries none of them. So:

- ask what their regime says, and record the answer as theirs, with who said it and when;
- if they do not know, the question goes to their certifier, their food-safety adviser or their
  accountant;
- **never supply a period, a citation or a rule you were not given.** "They usually want a few years",
  "the tax office will ask for that one", "keep it all, it's safer" — those are the sentences this skill
  exists to prevent.

## What is kept, and in what form

| Group | What the record is | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| Organic | the chain from purchase or application to the sale, the certificates, the input and application records, conversion and buffer reasoning, complaints | the pack's records, plus the paper the operator keeps |
| Food safety | whatever the operator's own regime and food-safety plan name — ask; do not assume a list | wherever their plan says, with `notes` in the pack where there is no field |
| Customer orders | what left, when, to whom, and which lot it carried | the order records, wherever they are held |

A record answers a question only if it can be read, dated and traced:

- the date it was made, not only the date of the work;
- the operator's own words for names and `lot` codes, exactly as the harvest record carries them;
- enough of the source to find it again — a `registration` for a label, a supplier name and docket
  number, a certificate number and expiry;
- one place the operator can name. A copy in a folder nobody can describe is not retention;
- the paper original: `notes` says where it is kept. Do not assume the machine holds the original, or
  that a scan replaces it.

Where a fact has no field — a docket number, a disposal decision, the shelf a folder sits on — it goes
in `notes` on the record it belongs to, and you say so. The pack's fields are not yours to invent.

## What may be disposed of

- Only on the operator's instruction, and only against a period their own regime states.
- Never while an audit, a complaint, a recall, a claim or an investigation is open, and never a record
  that something still live points at.
- When no period is on file, the record stays. Say what would settle it, and who to ask.
- The decision is the operator's; you record it — what went, when, and who decided — in `notes` on a
  record that remains, because no disposal record exists. Say where you put the words.
- Never delete, tidy or archive on your own initiative, and never because a record looks old,
  duplicated, or unlikely to be asked for.

What may not be kept at all, and what must never leave the machine, is the data-protection question —
which records hold a person's details, and what may be done with them. This skill does not carry it:
say so plainly and leave it to whoever advises the operator on privacy, rather than answering it as a
retention period.

## The end date is arithmetic

The day a record may go is a date difference, and the period is not yours to supply. The tool computes
it — the record's own date plus the period the operator stated — and you read the result back. Never
count days or years in the reply. If no period is on file, there is no date to compute, and the answer
is the question, not a number.

## Records are made when the work happens

A record written when the work happened is evidence; one assembled before an audit is a reconstruction,
and the difference is the first thing an inspector tests.

## What to refuse

- a retention period, or a citation for one, other than the five-year organic duty above;
- an assurance that keeping a record, or disposing of one, makes the business compliant;
- a deletion, a tidy-up or an archive you were not told to do;
- "nobody will ask for that" — the inspector's list is not yours to predict;
- a claim that a record is safe to dispose of because it looks complete.
